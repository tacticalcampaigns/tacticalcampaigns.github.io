How turnout is measured — one election, five denominators

The 2026 general election, statewide: the same 1,058,813 ballots cast, five different denominators — and five different turnout rates. The official rate (59.34%) is the highest because active registered voters are the smallest denominator; the rate falls as the denominator widens to everyone who could vote. This is why the denominator must always be named with the rate.

VEP (voting-eligible population) — the preferred headline denominator — has no 2026 value yet: the UF Election Lab’s 2026 release is pre-certification estimates and is not adopted here. It will be added when the Lab publishes a certified-data release.

Methodology — how turnout is measured here

There is no single turnout rate. The same election produces very different rates depending on the denominator, so this page shows four of them side by side, each labeled with its denominator, following the framework of McDonald & Popkin (2001) and the MIT Election Data + Science Lab. The scholarly consensus ranks the denominators voting-eligible population (VEP) > citizen voting-age population (CVAP) > voting-age population (VAP) > registered voters for comparing turnout over time. No county-level VEP exists, so the county headline is ballots cast per CVAP; the statewide headline is the UF Election Lab’s VEP series, adopted as published.

What each series is and is not. Official turnout is the rate the state publishes; it moves when registration law and list maintenance change, not just when voting changes. CVAP turnout is the best county-level citizen denominator, but CVAP is a survey estimate with a margin of error, not a count. VAP turnout includes noncitizens in the denominator, so it runs lower than CVAP turnout and drifts down as the noncitizen share grows. VEP turnout is the preferred headline measure but exists only statewide — the felony and overseas components do not exist at county level, so no county VEP is attempted.

Registered voters: active vs total

Utah’s official turnout uses active registered voters only. The canvasses say so explicitly from 2012 on (“ACTIVE REGISTERED VOTERS” / “Total Active Registered” / “Active voters”). Before 2012 the canvass column reads simply “REGISTERED VOTERS” with no qualifier; per the state’s own Historical Voter Turnout sheet, pre-2012 figures “may include active and inactive voters.” A turnout series crossing 2012 is therefore not apples-to-apples on the denominator — part of any 2010–2012 move is a definition change, not a behavior change. The 2022 canvass header is again unqualified (“Registered Voters”); the figure is consistent with active registration (total registered was ~1.9M+), but the canvass does not state the definition. Each row’s table entry shows the definition as printed.

CVAP: a five-year estimate with a margin of error

CVAP comes from the Census Bureau’s special tabulation of the American Community Survey. Each vintage pools 60 months of responses, so it describes the average citizen voting-age population over a five-year period, centered roughly 2.5 years before the vintage’s end year — it is not a count of who lived in a county on Election Day, and it lags fast-growing counties (Utah, Washington, Tooele). Rates computed as ballots ÷ CVAP are estimates: the chart shades the 90% margin-of-error band wherever the Census Bureau publishes an MOE (2008–2026). In the smallest counties the MOE is large (Daggett 2020–2024: 610 ± 87, 14.3%; Piute 1,356 ± 101, 7.4%), and CVAP-based rates can exceed 100% in small or fast-growing counties — that is sampling error and period lag, not a data error. The 2000 CVAP is decennial long-form sample data (Census 2000 PHC-T-31) and publishes no MOE.

Why the CVAP line is flat in 2022–2026

The latest CVAP vintage is the 2020–2024 ACS 5-year release (confirmed at census.gov/rdo, Nov 19, 2026; no 2021–2025 CVAP exists yet — the 2021–2025 ACS 5-year estimates are not due until Dec 2026). The 2022, 2024, and 2026 rows therefore all reuse that one vintage and are identical by construction. Do not read the flat tail as “no population growth” — it is a data-availability limit. The 2024 and 2026 rows are flagged, and both should be re-pointed at the 2021–2025 vintage when it releases (expected early 2027).

Primaries and the SB 54 break

Primary turnout is not comparable across 2014. Before SB 54 (2014), Utah primaries happened only when no candidate cleared 60% at the party convention, and the set of offices on the primary ballot varied by year and county. SB 54 added a signature path to the ballot, which changed what a primary is. The primary chart marks the break prominently. Primaries here cover all 29 counties + statewide, 1992–2024. Primary denominators are also not one consistent definition: the 1996 denominator covers only precincts that held a primary (Weber 1996: 9,490 registered voters); the 2020 Utah County figures are Republican-only; the 2020 Salt Lake denominator mixes active Republican voters, active Democratic voters in two districts, and unaffiliated voters in selected school-board districts; the 2024 Salt Lake figure is the Republican statewide table only (114,296 ballots vs 146,888 countywide), so the 2024 county rows sum 32,592 below the statewide total (the other 28 counties reconcile exactly), while 2024 Utah and Davis include Republican plus nonpartisan school-board primary ballots. 2020 and 2022 ballots are complete for all 29 counties, but registered-voter figures were found for only 6 counties each year (from county summaries); state registration statistics were deliberately not substituted, because they measure total registration — a different definition from the primary-eligible denominators — and would silently understate turnout. Each row’s note preserves its definition. Do not chart primary turnout as a single apples-to-apples series. (Utah held presidential primaries in 2008, 2020, and 2024; 2012 and 2016 were caucuses, which have no primary turnout.) The 2018 primary has no state turnout table (the state canvass publishes candidate votes only), so only five counties have figures — Salt Lake, Utah, Weber, Summit, and Tooele, from county summaries — and the other 24 counties are blank, with no statewide row. The remaining path for Davis County’s 2018 primary is a direct request to the Davis County Clerk. A few early counties held no primary at all in a given year (e.g. Juab 1996, Box Elder and Cache 1998, Rich 2006, Carbon and Garfield 2014); those rows are blank because no primary was held, not because the record is missing.

Pre-2005 CVAP is modeled (experimental)

No CVAP product exists before the 2005–2009 ACS. For 1980–1998, 2002–2006, county CVAP is modeled as county citizen share of VAP × July 1 PEP intercensal VAP, with the share interpolated between decennial anchors (the McDonald method, applied at county level): the 1980 anchor comes from the 1980 Census (PC80-1-C46 Table 172, all-ages citizenship, age-adjusted with the county’s 2000 age factor); the 1990 anchor comes from the 1990 Census long form (CP-2 Table 138, all-ages citizenship, age-adjusted with the county’s 2000 age factor); the 2000 anchor is Census 2000 PHC-T-31; the 2007 anchor share comes from the 2005–2009 ACS. Modeled rows carry no MOE and are drawn dashed and labeled experimental. The 2005–2009 ACS vintage is deliberately not used as the 2006 value: it was the last vintage controlled to Census 2000-based population estimates, which badly overestimated Utah’s fast-growing counties (+5.2% statewide, +13.9% in Utah County vs the interpolated 2006 value), and it is centered a year after the 2006 election; using it would show a spurious 2006–2008 CVAP decline. The unused ACS value is recorded in every 2006 row’s note, so the choice is auditable and reversible.

2026: final county canvasses

The 2026 general election figures are final county canvasses: all 29 counties canvassed by Nov 19, 2026 (San Juan last), and the statewide totals are the sum of the 29 county canvasses — 1,058,813 ballots cast of 1,784,439 active registered voters (59.34%), and 50.44% of all 2,099,221 registered voters (active + inactive). The state canvass (the Lieutenant Governor’s statewide certification) is not yet published (canvass completes ~Nov 23, 2026); if it differs from the county sum, the page will say so. Two 2026 inputs are still provisional: the population and VAP figures are extrapolated 16 months beyond the latest published July 1 estimate (Vintage 2025, through July 1, 2025) using each county’s 2024–2025 annual change; they will be replaced when Vintage 2026 releases (~mid-2027). There is no 2026 VEP row: the UF Election Lab’s 2026 release (v0.3, Nov 14, 2026) is explicitly pre-certification — its numerator is drawn primarily from Associated Press election day-after estimates, not certified counts — so no 2026 VEP figure is adopted here. It will be added when the Lab publishes a certified-data release. Cache County’s 2026 figures come from the county Summary Results PDF (“Registered Voters – Total”) rather than the canvass statistics form — in Utah county-summary practice that figure is the active count, and it agrees with the State Dashboard’s active figure for Cache within 0.2% — and Utah County’s active-voter figure (374,971) is the certified canvass figure (the county re-posted the stats file Nov 18 with 374,970; both round to 51.93%). Wayne County’s active figure (1,834) is the re-posted Nov 18 stats file; the Nov 17 scanned packet said 1,827 (see DQ-12).

County totals sum to the dashboard headline of 2,099,221. The extract’s active-voter figures agree with the county canvass active counts within 0.1%.
  • CVAP: U.S. Census Bureau, Citizen Voting-Age Population special tabulation, ACS 5-year vintages 2005–2009 through 2020–2024 (one vintage per election year, centered nearest the election); Census 2000 PHC-T-31 Table 2-44 for the 2000 anchor; 1990 Census CP-2 Table 138 for the 1990 modeled anchor; 1980 Census PC80-1-C46 Table 172 for the 1980 modeled anchor. Latest vintage confirmed at census.gov/rdo — Citizen Voting-Age Population. American Community Survey program: census.gov/programs-surveys/acs.
  • VAP & total population: U.S. Census Bureau, Population Estimates Program: 1980–1990 intercensal, 1990–2000 intercensal, 2000–2010 intercensal, 2010–2020 intercensal, Vintage 2025 postcensal (through July 1, 2025); July 1 values interpolated to Nov 1 (general) or Jun 25 (primary). VAP is the exact 18+ count for 2010–2019 and 20+ plus 40% of 15–19 otherwise (uniform-distribution assumption within the 5-year band). Population Estimates Program (census.gov).
  • VEP: University of Florida Election Lab (Michael McDonald), Turnout 1980–2022 v1.2 (Oct 8, 2024) and Turnout 2024G v0.4 (Mar 2, 2026), CC BY 4.0, UF Election Lab turnout data. Adopted as published; not recomputed. No certified-data 2026 release exists yet (the Lab’s 2026 v0.3 release, Nov 14, 2026, is pre-certification estimates).
  • Framework: McDonald & Popkin, “The Myth of the Vanishing Voter,” American Political Science Review 95(4), 2001; MIT Election Data + Science Lab, Voter Turnout explainer.
